For centuries, the river system has served as the backbone of Bangladesh's socio-economic development and ecological sustainability. From the Himalayan glaciers, then Teesta alluvial fan and the fertile floodplains to the Bay of Bengal, rivers shape Bangladesh's landscape as biggest asset as well as a challenge. Nevertheless, over last 15 years, government has paid less attention to river management while prioritising building unplanned roads and highways without adequate consideration of natural drainage systems. As a result, poor river management has become a vital issue that affects the society, the economy and ecology. Currently, the BNP government is taking a new approach to water management that seems synergic. Through two major projects such as the Teesta Comprehensive Management and Restoration Project in the north and the Padma Barrage in the southwest, the government is trying to consider Bangladesh's rivers as a single hydro grid system from the Teesta Fan to the Ganges Delta rather than isolated administrative headaches.

Actually, the project pinpoints the restoration of Teesta alluvial fan, a region that experiences extreme seasonal volatility at northern part of Bangladesh. During the dry season, the Teesta's flow drops and causes water shortages for agriculture and local communities. In contrast, the monsoon appears with powerful floods and riverbank erosion that damage farmland, homes and livelihoods. The proposed project aims to address these long-standing problems through integrated river management and restoration. With technical and financial support from the Chinese Government, Bangladesh plans to manage over 230 km of embankments and build a 224 km road network with water storage reservoirs and satellite socio-ecological entities. If the project is managed successfully, it will not only solve ecological challenges but also provide major economic benefits to the greater Rangpur and Rajshahi regions.

The Padma Barrage Project, recently approved for 34,497 crore BDT, is expected to conserve monsoon water for use during the dry season aiming to protect Ganges delta's ecosystem. The Padma Barrage, plans to stretch about 2.1 kilometres across the river and store nearly 2.9 billion cubic meters of monsoon runoff that supports irrigation, navigation and terrestrial ecology. The project proposes to store monsoon water and channelise fresh water to the tributaries and distributaries. By increasing freshwater flow, the barrage will support farming, safe drinking water and the Sundarbans ecosystem as well.

When viewed together, these two mega-projects create a strong synergy, especially in northern and south-western Bangladesh. A stable Teesta project can ensure year-round irrigation and help reduce riverbank erosion at Kurigram, Lalmonirhat, Gaibandha, Rangpur, Sirajganj and Bogra. On the other hand, the Padma Barrage can help reduce salinity and desertification chance in the southwest by proper channelised water management. By restoring downstream river system, the project can build a safeguard to the fragile Sundarbans mangrove ecosystem as well.

This dual-front approach points out a significant shift toward holistic spatio-temporal planning for better hydro management. However, infrastructures or physical projects alone cannot solve Bangladesh's river management challenges. As Bangladesh usually faces complex regional dynamics and moves forward with its river management plans, it must carefully balance Chinese support while maintaining strong eco-diplomatic understanding with India, Nepal and Bhutan. The 1996 Ganges Water Sharing Treaty will be expired in end of 2026, marking this an important timeline for Bangladesh's water security. While a Padma barrage can store and manage water at lower riparian, it cannot alone solve the dry-season water crisis if there is not adequate water flow from upper riparian. Therefore, domestic water projects should go hand in hand with better cooperation and data-driven policy across South Asia.

To ensure the long-term hydro management success, Bangladesh should treat its rivers as living systems rather than merely water channels. First, it must promote regional cooperation with neighbouring countries to manage shared rivers more effectively. According to Schumm's Fluvial System Model (1977), a river functions as single connected system- from source zone where water and sediment are originated, through the transport zone where sediments are carried, to the accumulation zone where they are finally deposited. Since Bangladesh is a downstream country mainly with transport and accumulation zone, so this entire hydro management strategy cannot be succeeded without cooperation from source zone's countries. Therefore, instead of focusing on short-term bilateral negotiations, Bangladesh should work with its neighbours to manage the basin-based river management system for long-term preparation. Secondly, engineering process must prioritise the balance of its complexities and ecological perspective focusing on low-turbidity hydraulic dredging and nature-based canal excavation to preserve benthic habitats, protect fish migration corridors and safeguards natural silt-deposition at the lower streams. Thus, sustainable river restoration is possible.

Ultimately, the government's focus on the Teesta Project and the Padma Barrage sets a prospective vision for future river management significantly. By recognising river as an interconnected system, his government marks eco-diplomacy with neighboring countries at the heart of Bangladesh's water strategy. Thereby, from the Teesta Fan to the Ganges delta, Bangladesh must build a holistic water management system that safeguards our rivers for future generations.
