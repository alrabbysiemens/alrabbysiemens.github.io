In Bangladesh, the Ganges River system is not merely a question of water-sharing; it is increasingly a matter of ecological survival, agricultural security and regional diplomacy.

Bangladesh is on the brink of one of its most pivotal hydro-political negotiations of the past decade as the 30-year Ganges Water Sharing Treaty expires in December 2026. The upcoming treaty renewal will not just be a diplomatic negotiation with India but also a significant trial of the BNP Government's eco-diplomacy where environmental security and regional diplomacy intersect.

The existing treaty signed between Bangladesh and India in 1996 was considered a major agreement at that time. It established a formal system to transfer dry season flow to the Farakka point for balancing competing national needs. It served as a guideline and first gave some hope to restore the hydrological system at the south-western part of Bangladesh. But over the years, there have been doubts in its operational efficiency as the rigid and formula-based allocation system has not always been implemented consistently or responsively by India.

As per 1996 treaty, if the flow of the river is below 70,000 cusec, then water will be equally shared by Bangladesh and India. The government agreed that Bangladesh would receive 35,000 cusecs when flows fall between 70,000 and 75,000 cusecs. If the flow is more than 75,000 cusecs, India will receive 40,000 cusecs with the rest of the water going to Bangladesh. The structure was optimistic but has faced serious operating issues in practice from the neighbour.

The representative hydrological data indicate that during critical periods, flows recorded at downstream monitoring stations – notably Hardinge Bridge – could fall to nearly 30,000 cusecs, with significant lag periods. These mismatches produce significant stress on agriculture systems and ecological systems in the adjacent part of Bangladesh. The case of Ganges-Kobadak (GK) Irrigation Project requires approximately 34,000 cusecs to operate it in a stably. In a few dry-season events, the recorded flow has fallen to as low as almost ~23,000 cusecs, which has led to temporary irrigation cessation across an estimated ~1.97 lakh hectares of agricultural areas in the south-western part of Bangladesh. These disruptions expose the gap between the treaty's stated framework and India's effective implementation of it.

One of the major drawbacks of the existing system is its limited consideration of water quantity allocation at a single fixed point such as Farakka. The next treaty should clearly depart from this model and consider a Basin-wide river governance approach. The Ganges-Padma system of rivers does not act as a single channel, but rather as a dynamic network of connected ecosystems, where upstream rainfall, sediment transport, floodplain dynamics and delta formation are all linked. Integrated monitoring of a river system is necessary for a basin-based approach that involves upstream rainfall patterns, groundwater-surface water interaction, sediment flow and seasonal discharge variations across the basin. Otherwise, the treaty may remain limited to water sharing rather than becoming a broader river governance framework.

A future treaty should establish a shared digital hydrological platform between Bangladesh and India integrating real-time hydro-climatic data flow and satellite observations to improve transparency and reduce uncertainty in water flow projections. At the same time, the concept of 'ecological flow' must become key to the treaty framework to sustain riverine ecosystems and resources and downstream ecosystems such as the Sundarbans and deltaic wetlands. Climate adaptation should also be embedded into treaty design through Scenario-based modeling using Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs), allowing more flexible and climate-responsive allocation mechanisms instead of rigid fixed formulas.

In this evolving context, the BNP Government faces perhaps the most significant hydropolitical challenge in recent decades. In the light of the above, the government will need to balance complex regional diplomacy with long-term environmental security and domestic water resilience. The recently proposed Padma Barrage project reflects an important strategic shift in that direction. Beyond addressing dry-season water shortages, the 2.1-kilometre barrage - designed to store nearly 2.9 billion cubic metres of water - could strengthen Bangladesh's internal water regulation capacity. This would allow Bangladesh to enter post-2026 treaty negotiations from a position of greater strength and resilience and more resilient position. At the same time, the project signals a broader transition from a narrow water-sharing approach toward integrated basin management combining diplomacy, domestic infrastructure, irrigation stability, and long-term environmental planning. The upcoming treaty negotiation therefore remains not only a diplomatic challenge, but also a test of whether the BNP Government can transform Bangladesh's river governance strategy into a more adaptive, science-based and nationally resilient framework, capable of protecting both environmental security and the millions of livelihoods dependent on the Ganges basin.

Ultimately, the 2026 negotiation represents far more than a routine treaty renewal. It is an opportunity to redefine the future philosophy of water governance in South Asia -- whether the region will move toward competitive resource politics or cooperative ecological stewardship. For Bangladesh, and particularly for the BNP Government, this is both a diplomatic responsibility and an environmental imperative.
