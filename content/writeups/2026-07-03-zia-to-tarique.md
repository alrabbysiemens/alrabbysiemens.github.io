Bangladesh today stands as a victim at the frontline of climate vulnerability.

From flash floods to disappearing wetlands, from dead canals to declining groundwater levels, from urban waterlogging to riverbank erosion, environmental challenges are becoming defining features of the country's future development plan.

In this context, the renewed focus on canal excavation and restoration has prompted an important historical discussion that connects environmental governance, public participation, and political vision.

Nearly five decades ago, president Ziaur Rahman initiated the famous khal kata kormoshuchi (Canal Excavation Project) across rural Bangladesh.

At first glance, the programme appeared to be a simple canal excavation campaign aimed at improving irrigation and agricultural productivity. Yet, beyond its physical infrastructure, the initiative carried a deeper philosophy that reflected the involvement of ordinary people in managing local environmental resources.

During the late 1970s, Bangladesh was still recovering from war, economic instability, food insecurity, and fragile rural infrastructure.

Environmental governance was not yet a globally established concept. The international discourse on sustainable development would gain momentum mainly after the 1980s, particularly following the Brundtland Report of 1987 and the Rio Earth Summit of 1992.

However, long before these frameworks became institutionalized worldwide, Bangladesh had already witnessed a grassroots-oriented environmental initiative through canal restoration and community mobilisation.

The plan was not only about digging the canals, but it was also about the widespread engagement that it generated.

The restoration of water flow, irrigation systems, and crop yields was a highly participatory process involving villagers, farmers, and local communities. In many places, waterways were used as canals both for transport and as a lifeline for rural ecosystems and livelihoods.

The evident presence of Ziaur Rahman with the locals represented development as a social responsibility, not merely an administrative task.

In present times, such methods are termed Community-Based Environmental Management (CBEM) or participatory environmental governance.

These frameworks prioritise community engagement, local governance, and environmental sustainability. While the excavation programme was not explicitly designed with these academic terms in mind, it did contain many of the core elements.

The operation could be considered a pioneering attempt in participatory environmental management in Bangladesh in hindsight.

The programme was launched at a time when most developing countries relied mainly on top-down development planning, demonstrating an understanding of how environmental sustainability and community well-being were interconnected.

Thus, the modern resurgence of canal excavation projects under Tarique Rahman has brought a new life to canals and has breathed new life into canal restoration, given that environmental problems are even more complex in 2026 than they were in the 1970s.

Climate change, extreme rainfall, urban flooding, wetland degradation, and ecological imbalance now threaten both rural and urban systems.

In such circumstances, restoring canals is no longer only an agricultural necessity; it is increasingly becoming an environmental management strategy.

However, the success of this revival will depend on whether it evolves beyond symbolic excavation into a genuinely participatory and scientifically grounded environmental programme.

According to government statements, the initiative has already been framed within a long-term national environmental restoration strategy.

As part of BNP's electoral commitment, a five-year master plan has reportedly been developed to re-excavate nearly 20,000 kilometers of canals and water bodies across the country. Under the first phase of the pilot programme, approximately 1,000 kilometers of canal excavation are expected to be completed within 180 days.

Policymakers believe the project will generate immediate, visible benefits for local communities, particularly in strengthening agricultural productivity, improving fisheries, and restoring ecological balance. The programme is also viewed as a significant step toward revitalising Bangladesh's aquatic ecosystems and enhancing climate resilience through water-centered environmental governance.

Most importantly, local communities must once again become custodians of these environmental systems. Union Parishads, farmers, youth groups, local water committees, researchers, and civil society organisations should all be integrated into planning and monitoring processes.

Scientific tools such as GIS mapping, remote sensing, hydrological analysis, and climate risk assessment must also complement community participation.

The future of Bangladesh's waterways cannot be based only on momentary political attention. Sustainable restoration necessitates continued maintenance and ecological awareness.

Canals are not isolated drainage trenches; they are part of a complex hydrological and environmental network that affects agriculture, biodiversity, groundwater recharge, drainage systems, and wetland habitats.

Ultimately, the significance of revisiting the participatory environmental governance philosophy today lies precisely here.

It reminds us that environmental governance is most effective when it connects people, ecology, and development.

What began decades ago as a rural canal excavation campaign holds important lessons for climate resilience in the twenty-first century.

From Zia to Tarique, the return of participatory environmental governance offers Bangladesh an opportunity not only to restore canals but also to restore the relationship between communities and the environment itself.
